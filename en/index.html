Why it matters

Reaction time is the foundation of almost every sport — goals, defense, and getting past an opponent are decided in fractions of a second. Regular reaction training shortens the gap between perception and movement and reduces the number of missed moments.

What it measures

How fast you react to a simple visual stimulus — the time between the color change and your click, measured in milliseconds.

Who it helps

Athletes across all disciplines, drivers, and anyone who wants to track their psychomotor performance over time.

For comparison: the average adult sits around 250 ms · professional esports players 150–180 ms · Olympic sprinters at the start 120–160 ms (a reaction under 100 ms counts as a false start).

In practice

Where visual and cognitive training is actually used

Visual and cognitive training isn't a marketing buzzword — it's used worldwide across sport, safety, and medicine.

Sport

From the steering wheel to the pitch — peripheral vision and fast decision-making matter in almost every sport.

Motorsport

F1 and WRC drivers have to assess a situation in a split second at speeds over 300 km/h.

Choice Reaction Peripheral Vision Trajectory Prediction Hand-Eye Coordination Saccadic Recognition

Football

A study by J. Faubert (2013) showed that professional footballers learn to track multiple moving objects at once faster than the general population. Olympic medalist goalkeeper Erin McLeod went through similar training.

Multi-Object Tracking Peripheral Vision Trajectory Prediction Choice Reaction

Basketball (NBA)

Stars like Stephen Curry and Kawhi Leonard have experimented with visual training. NBA clubs, including the Toronto Raptors, commonly use NeuroTracker.

Multi-Object Tracking Peripheral Vision Task Switching Hand-Eye Coordination

American football (NFL)

Former MVP Matt Ryan trains with NeuroTracker year-round. NFL teams also routinely test players on devices like Dynavision — reacting to light cues across the full field of view.

Peripheral Vision Trajectory Prediction Go/No-Go Test Saccadic Recognition

Hockey

Connor McDavid has spent years training peripheral vision and reaction time — according to his agent, it gives him a critical edge on the ice. Sidney Crosby and Tom Wilson trained similarly.

Peripheral Vision Choice Reaction Multi-Object Tracking

Combat sports & fencing

Telling a real attack from a feint happens in tens of milliseconds — exactly what cognitive control measures.

Go/No-Go Test Choice Reaction Contrast Sensitivity Concentration Grid

Health

Visual training isn't just about performance — it also plays a role in brain health and recovery.

Surgery

Research (Grantcharov et al.) showed that visuospatial ability predicts performance in laparoscopic surgery.

Hand-Eye Coordination Peripheral Vision

ADHD

EndeavorRx — a video game designed to improve attention in children with ADHD — became the first game of its kind to receive FDA approval, in 2020.

Go/No-Go Test Task Switching

Dementia prevention

The large ACTIVE study (Edwards et al.) showed that speed-of-processing training — the same principle behind UFOV — nearly halved dementia risk over a 10-year follow-up.

Peripheral Vision Saccadic Recognition

Injury recovery

Oculomotor exercises — tracking, saccades, smooth pursuit — are a standard part of sports concussion rehabilitation protocols.

Smooth Pursuit Saccadic Recognition

Esports

Professional players reach reaction times around 150–200 ms, well below the population average.

Aviation

Pilot selection and training routinely includes Useful Field of View-type tests — situational awareness is a matter of safety here.

Road safety

UFOV is one of the strongest predictors of crash risk — in the US it's part of fitness-to-drive assessments for older drivers.

Gaming research

The classic Green & Bavelier (2003) study showed that action video game players have an expanded field of view and faster visual processing.
