Click the color the word is printed in — not the color it names.

Why it matters

Your brain reads words faster than it recognizes colors. When these two processes conflict, you get the so-called Stroop effect — and the ability to suppress the automatic but wrong response in favor of the correct one is the basis of executive control. It's the exact same skill an athlete needs to ignore an opponent's fake move and react to the real signal.

What it measures

Cognitive inhibition — the ability to suppress an automatic response (reading the word) in favor of a task that demands more attention (naming the ink color).

Who it helps

Athletes facing deceptive cues (fakes, direction changes), and anyone who wants to sharpen focus and resistance to distraction.

Rating scale

≥ 95%Exceptional control
85–94%Excellent control
70–84%Average control
< 70%Below average control

The test is based on the classic Stroop test (Stroop, 1935) — one of the most widely used tools in psychology for measuring cognitive inhibition. The reaction-time gap between matching and mismatching cues (the Stroop effect) is typically 100–200 ms even in healthy adults — the smaller that gap, the better your cognitive control.
